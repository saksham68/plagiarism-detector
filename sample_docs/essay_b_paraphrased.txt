The rise of distributed systems has fundamentally changed how engineers
think about reliability. In a single machine, failure is binary: the
process is running or it has crashed. In a distributed system, failure is
partial. Some nodes may be up while others are down, and the network
connecting them may be slow, congested, or entirely partitioned. This
partial failure model is what makes distributed systems so difficult to
reason about.

Consensus protocols like Paxos and Raft exist precisely to hide this
complexity from application developers. They allow a cluster of machines to
agree on a single value even when some of the machines crash or the network
misbehaves, so long as a majority of nodes remain reachable. This majority
requirement, often called a quorum, is the foundation of most highly
available systems built today.

Understanding these tradeoffs is essential for any engineer building
systems that need to survive real-world failure conditions, and not just
the happy path tested in a local development environment.
